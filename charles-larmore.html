The self and self-understanding

In The Practices of the Self, Larmore examines how people understand themselves and make sense of their lives. His work provides a philosophical point of entry for this project’s questions about consciousness, identity, and agency.

Read alongside Westworld, these ideas help us ask how memory and reflection shape a sense of self. The comparison is a way to explore the series’ questions, not a claim that its fictional hosts prove a particular theory of consciousness.

Exploring The Practices of the Self

Larmore’s account connects selfhood to an unfolding life: we understand ourselves through the relationships among our experiences, desires, commitments, and reflections—not by following an unchanging script.

The self and personal identity

Larmore’s rope metaphor offers a way to think about how a person can change and remain the same person. A rope is made from strands that overlap and intertwine; no single strand has to run from one end to the other for the rope to hold together. Likewise, continuity of the self does not require one belief, memory, or goal to remain unchanged throughout a life. Larmore describes this continuity as “woven like a rope—by the partial intertwining of its various strands, without any one of them extending from beginning to end” (175).

This account makes personal identity a matter of connections across a life, rather than perfect sameness at every moment. Understanding how the past connects to the present helps explain a person’s continuing identity (172). A change in what someone values need not erase who they were; it can become another strand in a life connected to, but not identical with, what came before.

Desires and commitments

Desires matter to the self because they can motivate thought and action. Commitments give those motivations a more lasting shape: they express what a person cares about and help connect one choice to another through reasons. As commitments develop, a person’s understanding of what is good can develop with them. The self is therefore neither a fixed inventory of preferences nor a completely detached observer of its own motives; it takes shape through the concerns that guide a life and the ways those concerns change.

Reflection and revision

Reflection allows a person to respond to a difficulty by considering their own reactions and reasons. Larmore characterizes “the character of reflection in general” as “the response to a problem” (186). Reflection is not necessarily a view from nowhere: it begins within a life already shaped by feelings and commitments. By examining those commitments, a person can question or revise them without needing to treat every change as the loss of a self.

A good life beyond a fixed plan

Larmore challenges the idea that living well means controlling the whole shape of one’s life in advance. A plan can guide action, but it cannot anticipate every possibility that experience may disclose. He writes, “we live well when we are not only active but passive too,” emphasizing both deliberate choice and openness to what arrives unexpectedly (180). The unforeseen may reveal “new vistas of meaning” and make existing goals “appear in a different light” (180).

This openness is not simply drifting without direction. It recognizes that what a person has reason to value may emerge through living, rather than being fully settled beforehand. Larmore’s claim that the good is “not defined in advance” (189) helps explain why a thoughtful life needs room for experience to reshape its aims. Conscious reflection can then help a person interpret what has changed and decide how to act, while acknowledging that not every valuable possibility could have been planned in advance.
