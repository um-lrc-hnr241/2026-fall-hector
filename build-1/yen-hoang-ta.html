Build 1: Planning and Consciousness

“My objection to the idea of a life plan sets out at a deeper level. The problem is not the vulnerability of our plans, but the drawbacks there can be in seeking to conform to a plan, especially when our life as a whole becomes its object. An essential dimension of the human good eludes us if we believe that our attitude toward life should be one of foresight and control, as the notion of a life plan assumes. On the contrary, we live well when we are not only active but passive too. There is an openness to life’s surprises that we do well to maintain. For the unforeseen, far from always being the mishap that makes our plans fall through, is sometimes the stroke of luck that opens up new vistas of meaning, forms of happiness and understanding that we never expected, whose existence we hardly suspected, and that not only can make our existing goals appear in a different light but can change our lives and self-conceptions in the most thoroughgoing ways.”

Charles Larmore, The Practices of the Self, 180

Charles Larmore, a philosopher, is the author of the book The Practices of the Self. In a part of this book, he discusses consciousness and whether it contributes to the happiness of people’s lives. He argues that to be able to obtain a good life, people should not “control everything” nor “just drift” their life plan. Their choices should be somewhere in the middle. Larmore believed that while having the goals of life, being open to unexpected experiences that might create disruption in the plan is also essential. This raises a question: if something we did not choose can change what we want, does consciousness create the change, or does it only respond after something else has already changed us?

By saying that the problem is not the weakness of plans but the attempt to conform to them, Larmore redirects the reader from a simple problem about unpredictability to a deeper problem about control. People with a designated and restricted plan may pass up the unexpected goods of life. Larmore suggests that our present reasoning cannot know every valuable possibility that future experience may reveal. This creates a limit for conscious planning, because consciousness can only work with what is already available to it. The author uses Julien Sorel as an example later in the reading (196). Julien changes his whole original plan, which is to increase his social placement, after the new situation happens. He recognizes his feelings for Madame de Rênal and a different kind of happiness. Larmore uses Julien to show that a person can discover something valuable that their earlier plan could not have predicted. Thus, it supports the idea that conscious planning is limited because it can only work with the goals and values a person already understands.

Larmore uses two opposite words when he argues that “We live well when we are not only active but passive too” (180) to suggest how a person can have a good life. Active means planning, choosing, and determining their own ending. In contrast, passive means receiving what comes to them, not seeking it. However, he did not separate them. He advises the reader to maintain (active) openness to surprises (passive). Therefore, consciousness has a role not only in choosing what happens next, but also in responding to surprising experiences that come later.

Consciousness still plays an important role even when the situation changes. Larmore writes that “the unforeseen” can open “new vistas of meaning” and make our goals “appear in a different light” (180). The quote shows why this influence is limited; these new opportunities come from the unforeseen experience, not from prior planning. The experience gives a person something new to consider, while consciousness helps the person understand and decide what to do. Therefore, consciousness can guide what happens next without creating the new possibilities.

Larmore’s argument makes me question what it really means for a choice to be “mine.” If something can change my goals and my “self-conception”, then I may understand myself differently from when I made the original plan. Saying consciousness creates the change by itself is different from saying it recognizes and decides the next action. Larmore does not clearly separate the cause of a new value from the conscious decision that follows it. The remaining question is whether recognizing and accepting a new reason is enough for consciousness to begin a new causal chain; in other words, to become the cause of new action rather than simply responding to what already happened.
