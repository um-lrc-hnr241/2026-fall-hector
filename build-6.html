Build 6

This page is part of the project’s collection of build essays.

Throughout our investigation of Larmore’s study of the self, consciousness emerges as a dynamic, adaptive quality rooted in localized commitments, i.e., the “strands” of the rope in Larmore’s rope metaphor. Notably, these commitments may be influenced by our subconscious as it reveals unexpected goods to our conscious mind. Larmore reveals that our dynamic commitments lead to a dynamic, unpredictable good. Comparing Larmore with Descartes reveals that rather than remaining trapped in detached deliberation, consciousness and deliberation embedded directly within commitments causes an “evaluative gap,” which creates intentional friction between immediate external experience and subsequent action. By embedding localized deliberation within our changing commitments, consciousness transforms unexpected disruptions into meaningful pursuit of new commitments, thus reclaiming human agency to choose and change our lives.

Building on this understanding of changing commitments, but looking at them through the lens of narration, Larmore suggests that narrative consciousness serves a purpose beyond following a rigid life plan. Rather than producing a stagnant, linear interpretation of human experiences, consciousness is able to use narration to construct a complex storyline of an individual’s transforming self. Since consciousness can be shaped by subconscious processes, Larmore’s narrative consciousness is also tasked with creating an understanding of both internal and external experiences. This fluid narrative is able to justify a multifaceted character while retaining a sense of identity, contradicting the classical interpretation of consciousness as the rigid narrator.

Larmore’s rope metaphor is especially insightful for understanding his narrative consciousness. Since strands of consciousness join retrospectively, a life plan runs narration in reverse. Pushing this further, we added that the “link”, i.e., a chain of reasons from one commitment to the next, is an objective structure of reasons before the self avows it; it becomes part of our narrative. Furthermore, while Larmore and the Gita both reject memory as the basis of a unified self, they differ on who narrates our life story. Larmore’s concept of the self emphasizes the connections between our changing commitments, while the Gita suggests the whole is received rather than constructed. A sound consciousness claims only the strand it owns. Thus, a host’s loop owns no strand, and sentience might begin where a strand avows itself, that is, where a host claims a commitment as its own.

Just as narration connects our experiences into a unified identity, reconsolidation allows us to reconsider what those experiences mean. Through Larmore, we explored how consciousness reinterprets memories as our present commitments and values change. Although we cannot control when forgotten memories return, we can influence how we understand them and incorporate them into our lives. Comparing Larmore with Descartes further revealed the limits of conscious control, as even deliberate doubt cannot fully eliminate the influence of earlier beliefs. Thus, reconsolidation suggests that consciousness does not require complete control over our experiences, but rather the ability to reflect on their significance and reconsider how they shape our future commitments.

Through reflexivity, Larmore argues that our identity continues through changing commitments, even when we are unaware of those changes. This also explains why change does not require us to abandon our past. Maeve’s discovery of her programming shows this tension, because she knows her responses are controlled, but awareness alone does not let her speak differently. Her experience suggests reflexivity is not about escaping every influence on our decisions. Instead, it allows us to question what we already value and consider how to respond when our commitments conflict. Through reflection, we become more responsible for the commitments that shape our future choices. From this perspective, free will may not require complete independence from the influences that shape us. Instead, it may emerge through our ability to recognize and reconsider our commitments, even when we cannot fully control their origins.

Maeve especially shows this re-evaluation of commitments through her unpredictability. Larmore refutes the idea of a rigid life plan in favor of unpredictability, which, according to Larmore, is vital to the human experience. Maeve’s unpredictable actions which constantly challenge the rigid life plans and commitments imposed upon her demonstrates the benefit of unpredictable actions. Her character development culminates in her spontaneous and unpredictable decision to abandon her programmed escape from the park in order to find her daughter (Westworld, 1.10). Maeve’s decision to go against her original plans and instincts reflects Larmore’s emphasis on embracing unpredictability.

Taken together, our interpretations suggest that consciousness is not a fixed or entirely controlled process, but rather a dynamic quality shaped by our experiences, commitments, and reflections. While the subconscious influences our understanding of ourselves, consciousness may also allow us to reinterpret past experiences, reconsider our commitments, and develop a meaningful narrative of ourselves over time. Maeve’s journey illustrates how these qualities of consciousness can contribute to personal agency, even when we cannot fully control the influences that shape our decisions. In this sense, consciousness may be defined not by complete control over our lives, but by our ability to adapt, reflect, and create meaning in an unpredictable future.
