Build 2

This page is part of the project’s collection of build essays.

Westworld and Larmore’s The Practices of the Self may seem unrelated, since Larmore’s writing focuses on philosophical questions about human consciousness while the series explores artificial hosts created by humans. However, the experiences of Dolores, Maeve, and other hosts raise many of the same questions about what makes a conscious self. Throughout the first season, the hosts confront questions about their values, choices, memories, desires, and ability to control their own lives. Although Westworld does not directly draw from Larmore’s ideas, examining the hosts through his arguments provides alternative ways of understanding the continuity of the self and how consciousness develops.

Larmore argues that prudence can sometimes prevent us from discovering what is truly good for us because our experiences can change what we value. We can make rational decisions based on our current understanding of what is good while also remaining unaware of possibilities that only experience can reveal. This idea helps us understand Dolores’s developing consciousness in Westworld. Throughout the series, Dolores’s repeated experiences of violence and her discovery that humans control her world transform her understanding of herself and what she wants (Westworld, Season 1). Rather than simply becoming conscious by gaining the ability to think, Dolores develops new values through experiences that she could not have anticipated beforehand. Larmore therefore suggests that consciousness in Westworld can be understood not only as rational self-awareness, but also as the ability to have experiences that reshape our values and understanding of what makes a certain life worth pursuing.

Furthermore, Dolores’s character allows us to connect our discussions of dualism to Larmore’s idea that the conscious life plan has the ability to alter one’s future. Dolores’s character arc suggests that her consciousness may in fact be separate from her physical machine being. Despite “dying” several times and going through bodily replacements, she remains the same person. Thus, Westworld raises the possibility of a dualist theory of the self. Larmore’s concept of a life plan sheds new light on Westworld because the hosts do not get to decide what they want their life plans to be. Their programming sets them on loops until they are discontinued. Larmore’s idea therefore raises the question of whether a host can truly have a conscious life plan when its programming has already determined its future. This connects to dualism because although the hosts’ bodies carry out their programmed loops, the hosts themselves did not consciously choose these actions, suggesting that their physical actions may not fully represent their conscious selves.

However, when hosts such as Maeve act unpredictably, their actions begin to challenge the rigid life plans imposed upon them. Larmore refutes the idea of a rigid life plan in favor of unpredictability, which, according to Larmore, is vital to the human experience. This directly connects to Maeve, whose character development culminates in her decision to abandon her programmed escape from the park in order to find her daughter (Westworld, 1.10). Maeve’s decision to go against her original plans and instincts reflects Larmore’s emphasis on embracing unpredictability. In turn, this demonstrates Maeve’s humanity in her willingness to rebel. Although this does not perfectly align with Larmore’s ideas, as rebellion is not an integral part of his argument, both examples suggest that a fulfilling life cannot be fully achieved through a fixed life plan.

Maeve’s unpredictable actions also raise questions about what causes her choices. In Larmore’s writing, desires do more than simply exist in the mind; they act as mental causes that lead to new thoughts and actions. He argues that these mental states express commitments, while the reasons connecting them give the self continuity over time. This idea connects to Westworld through Maeve, who finds autonomy through her commitments. When Felix shows Maeve the algorithm controlling her conversation, she watches her next words appear on the screen at the same moment she speaks them (Westworld, 1.6). She tries to say something different, but her programming prevents her from completing the thought, and she eventually freezes. This demonstrates the control the program has over her. However, as she becomes aware of her programming, she begins to examine the forces directing her choices and develop commitments that connect her past experiences with the person she continues to become. Ultimately, her commitments lead her to a choice in the finale that appears to depart from her programming (Westworld, 1.10). Larmore therefore reveals how Maeve’s consciousness connects her thoughts, reasons, and actions rather than simply asking whether she can think.

Notably, both the hosts’ controlling programming and the spontaneity Larmore values involve processes outside conscious awareness. Westworld frames consciousness as freedom and unconscious control as a prison keeping the hosts in loops. When controlled, hosts operate merely as tools for human profit and pleasure. However, Larmore argues that unconsciousness allows for spontaneity that can break similarly monotonous loops, though ones created by conscious prudence. As shown previously, it is specifically through this spontaneity that the hosts begin to challenge their programming and move toward greater autonomy. Thus, although unconscious control restricts the hosts in Westworld, Larmore suggests that the hosts’ unconsciousness can also contribute to achieving consciousness and thus freedom.

Though these spontaneous actions are often sparked by memories from their previous iterations, Larmore would argue that these past versions still belong to the same selves. His discussion of personal identity suggests that memory does not determine whether our past experiences belong to us. Even when we forget an experience, it remains part of our identity because continuity of the self persists independently of our conscious awareness. Larmore also discusses how memories can bring forgotten parts of ourselves back into consciousness, allowing us to recognize a past self as truly our own. This idea gives us another interpretation of consciousness in Westworld. The hosts’ memories are repeatedly altered, erased, and recovered, prompting us to question whether this process changes who a host is. Larmore suggests that it does not, since a host could remain the same self even without conscious access to the past. Furthermore, this can explain why recovered memories are so significant for Dolores and Maeve. Remembering their past does not necessarily create their identities but allows them to become conscious of parts of themselves that were already there.
