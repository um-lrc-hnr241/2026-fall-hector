Build 3: Reflexivity and Consciousness

Larmore believed consciousness is limited because people can only plan with the goals and values they already understand. At the end of Build 1, I questioned whether consciousness actually causes a change in what a person values or whether it only recognizes a change after experience has already produced it. This question leads directly to one of the qualities of consciousness: reflexivity. Reflexivity is the awareness that an individual is conscious of their thoughts and feelings about something that happened. I argued that reflexivity is important not because it prevents our first reactions, but because it allows us to question the reasons and commitments behind them, while still being aware that we are the same “we” even when our beliefs change.

According to Larmore, “the character of reflection in general…being the response to a problem” (186). In some situations, people do not think before they react. Feelings naturally respond, especially with negative emotions such as shame, anger, or defensiveness. Because these emotions involve how we see ourselves, they often expose a conflict between our reaction and what we value. Reflexivity examines the consciousness after the first reaction: it lets us notice what we felt and ask why, rather than something that simply happens to us.

This leads to the question of whether reflexivity supports questioning our commitments without ending the self that questions them. Larmore’s comparison of the self to a rope helps me to illustrate his idea about the continuity of a self (175). A rope has many overlapping strands. Each strand is thin and short, so no strand runs from beginning to end. Those strands are similar to the fragmented pieces of our life, such as our memories, beliefs, goals, or values. Being the same self does not mean having the same thoughts forever. If continuity required one unbroken strand (like having a goal for all your life), then reflexivity would threaten the self, since revising that goal would cut that strand. Instead, the new goal that results through the reflexivity process just grows out of the commitments that were already there.

The rope image explains how someone can change their commitments and still recognize their past self. For example, I played organ for my church because I wanted to practice my skills. But after a while, I developed an interest in composing music, not just playing the instrument. Reflexivity explains why there is a new strand in the rope. Larmore states that understanding how the past connects to the present can explain the sameness (172). Through reflexivity, the person can see both what has changed and what still ties them to who they were.

Reflexivity reveals that awareness helps recognize how our commitments change. It helps me to understand the transition from my past to my present self. Therefore, I think the importance of reflexivity is not that it keeps us the same forever, but that it helps us understand how we can change while still being the same self.

Maya Marie Thornton
