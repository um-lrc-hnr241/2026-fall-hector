The Narrative

In Westworld, awakening is a change in how a life is understood: experience that once served a script becomes memory a host can recognize as its own.

Experience, memory, and agency

The hosts are built to repeat narratives. Their routines make the park legible and entertaining to its human guests, while their memories of painful events are designed to be erased. Season one unsettles that arrangement when traces of experience persist. The hosts’ pasts begin to affect their present, even when the park’s designers intend each new loop to start clean.

That persistence matters because a self is not only a collection of thoughts observed from the outside. A person’s understanding of who they are is bound up with interpreting experience, recognizing continuity, and acting in light of what matters. Larmore’s work on the self gives us a way to frame this issue: self-consciousness involves a perspective on one’s own life, not simply the possession of information about oneself.

A story written by someone else

The hosts’ situation also makes agency difficult to separate from power. Their choices occur within a world designed by others, and their roles are repeatedly constrained by code, memory control, and the expectations of park guests. When a host begins to question the narrative assigned to them, the question is not only whether they can think, but whether they can act on an understanding of their own experience.

The comparison with Larmore is a philosophical lens, not a claim that fictional hosts settle what consciousness is. It helps the project ask how self-understanding and agency relate, and whether a life can feel like one’s own when its conditions have been authored by someone else.
